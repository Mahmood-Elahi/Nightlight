Why Nightlight

When a guess stops the search.

Diagnostic overshadowing and stereotyping of Indigenous patients in Canadian emergency departments, one form of bias in Canadian healthcare.

What goes wrong

A stereotype about alcohol or substance use can become the first guess in the ER, and that guess can stop the search for other causes. Conditions like diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) can look like intoxication, and get missed or delayed. Waiting can be fatal.

It affects First Nations and other Indigenous patients. British Columbia’s In Plain Sight report documented Indigenous-specific racism in health care.

A made-up story, for illustration

2:07 a.m.

A First Nations patient with type 1 diabetes is vomiting, drowsy, and getting more confused. Their clinic opens at eight. They remember being asked in the ER whether they’d been drinking. So they wait.

Why people wait

Fear of the first question

They expect to be assumed drunk, and not believed.

A bad experience before

Their own, or a family member’s.

Being alone

Long waits, unfamiliar faces, no one to speak up.

The clinic is closed

It opens at eight, and the ER isn’t a community’s own.

These reasons are illustrative, and we are confirming them with community partners. For clinical background on DKA, see the Canadian Journal of Emergency Medicine article.

How it works

From home to the ER, with someone beside you.

Three parts, built on the trust people already have in their clinic.

  1. Night Circle

    A nurse or health worker from your own clinic team answers by phone or text, overnight. They help you decide whether to go in tonight and stay on the line. They never diagnose, and emergencies go straight to 911.

  2. Handoff

    With your permission, your clinic calls the ER charge nurse before you arrive. You choose what is shared, so the first words in the chart are medical, not an assumption.

  3. Bedside support

    A companion meets you at the door and stays. One tap on “I don’t feel heard” requests a second clinician review.

  4. Next-day check-in

    Your clinic calls to see how you are, and asks how heard you felt. Ratings go to your community’s review group first.

Good to know

Is it free?

Yes. Patients never pay. Health systems and funders pay for the service.

Is anything recorded?

No. Sharing is off by default, and you choose what your clinic tells the ER.

Does it replace 911?

No. In an emergency, call 911. The Night Circle never diagnoses and sends emergencies straight to 911.

Do I have to use it?

No. Not using Nightlight never affects your care.

Is there any AI?

No. There is no AI in the patient’s experience. It is people: your clinic team and a companion.

For ER and clinic teams

A small ask. A safer first moment.

The ER’s part is a short call and a one-line note at triage.

The call-ahead

“Incoming: patient with type 1 diabetes, vomiting for two days, now confused. Please check glucose and ketones on arrival.” Shared only with consent.

The second look

If a patient taps “I don’t feel heard,” the charge nurse and companion are alerted and a second clinician review is requested, using existing senior clinicians.

The champion

A named clinical champion owns the process. Clinician judgment is never replaced.

Pilot: one clinic and one Calgary ER, evenings and overnight, framed as a patient-safety quality-improvement project.

How we’ll know it works

  • Overnight visits by people who would have waited
  • Time to correct diagnosis
  • Intoxication labels later changed
  • Patients who leave without being seen
  • Whether patients felt heard

Safeguards

  • Opt-in, off by default, nothing recorded
  • Indigenous partners co-lead in paid roles, with OCAP® guiding data decisions
  • Legal review under Alberta health privacy law before any pilot
  • Overnight coverage shared across clinics to avoid burnout

Questions from teams

Does this add work for the ER?

About a 30-second call and a one-line note per patient who opts in. A clinical champion owns the process.

How is this different from what exists?

Services like health lines such as 811 and ER liaison programs exist. Nightlight is your own clinic’s team, overnight, and it calls ahead. We are confirming which programs overlap so we can build on them.

What about clinical responsibility?

The Night Circle never diagnoses and refers emergencies to 911. A legal and clinical review will set its exact scope before any pilot.

Pilot & funding

Help keep the light on.

To move from prototype to pilot, we need the right partners, funding, and time.

Who we need

First Nations and Métis health partners and clinics, one Calgary ER with a clinical champion, the health authority, and funders.

What it costs

About $60K CAD for the pilot: overnight nurse staffing, training, phone system, evaluation, paid partner roles.

How long

Months 0–2: co-design. Months 3–8: pilot. Months 9–10: evaluate and report to the community first.

About us

No one should stay home because they fear they won’t be believed.

Our mission is an emergency room that communities trust, where every patient is believed, heard, and safe, at any hour.

Trust before technology

People come first: a familiar voice, a companion, a phone call.

Led with the community

Indigenous partners co-lead from day one, in paid roles, guided by OCAP® principles.
